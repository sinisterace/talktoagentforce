SAFE HOMEOWNER TROUBLESHOOTING

These are the only checks we ask a customer to perform themselves. Everything else
requires a technician. A customer should never open a sealed refrigerant circuit,
work inside an electrical panel beyond resetting a breaker once, bypass a safety
switch, or relight a pilot on equipment that smells of gas.

THERMOSTAT IS BLANK OR UNRESPONSIVE

Check whether the display is completely dark or showing a low-battery symbol.
Replace the batteries if the thermostat takes them; most take two AA batteries
behind the faceplate. If the thermostat is hardwired and blank, check the furnace
or air handler's service switch, which looks like a normal light switch on or near
the unit and is often flipped off accidentally. Then check the breaker labeled for
the furnace, air handler, or air conditioner. Reset a tripped breaker once. If it
trips again, stop and call us — a repeatedly tripping breaker indicates a fault,
not a nuisance trip.

Many furnaces also have a small door safety switch and a blocked-drain float
switch that cut power when the access panel is off or the condensate pan is full.
Confirm the access panel is fully seated.

NO COOLING BUT THE FAN RUNS

Check the filter first. A filter that is heavily loaded will restrict airflow
enough to freeze the indoor coil, and a frozen coil stops cooling. Look at the
refrigerant line where it enters the house; if it is coated in ice, turn the
system to off but leave the fan on, and let it thaw for two to three hours before
calling. Running a system with a frozen coil can damage the compressor.

Also confirm the outdoor unit is running and is not blocked by leaves, grass
clippings, or a cover left on from winter. Keep two feet of clearance on all sides.

NO HEAT BUT THE THERMOSTAT IS CALLING FOR HEAT

Confirm the thermostat is set to heat and the setpoint is above the current room
temperature. Confirm the furnace service switch is on and the breaker is not
tripped. On a gas furnace, look for a small indicator light behind the access
panel; a steady light usually means normal, and a repeating blink pattern is a
diagnostic code worth reporting to us because it tells the technician what to
bring. If there is any smell of gas at all, stop, leave the building, and call 911
or the gas utility from outside.

For an oil or propane system, check that the tank is not empty. This is a common
and non-obvious cause of a no-heat call.

WATER AROUND THE INDOOR UNIT

A small amount of water in the condensate pan is normal in cooling season. Water
overflowing the pan or on the floor usually means a clogged condensate drain. Turn
the system off to prevent further water damage and call us. Do not pour drain
cleaner into a condensate line.

SYSTEM RUNS CONSTANTLY AND NEVER REACHES SETPOINT

In extreme weather this can be normal — most residential systems are sized to hold
about twenty degrees of difference from outdoor temperature. Confirm the filter is
clean, all supply registers are open, and return vents are not blocked by
furniture. If the system is still short of setpoint by more than three degrees in
moderate weather, it needs service.

UNUSUAL NOISES AND WHAT THEY USUALLY MEAN

A short metallic click at startup and shutdown is normal relay operation. A
rhythmic squeal often indicates a belt or bearing. A loud bang at ignition on a
gas furnace can indicate delayed ignition and should be reported promptly. A
grinding or screeching from the outdoor unit usually indicates a failing fan motor
bearing. Rattling often means a loose panel or debris in the outdoor unit.

FILTERS

Most residential systems use a one-inch filter that should be replaced every one
to three months. Homes with pets or with construction dust need the shorter
interval. Four- and five-inch media filters are typically replaced every six to
twelve months. A filter with a very high MERV rating can restrict airflow on older
equipment; MERV 8 to MERV 11 is a reasonable range for most residential systems.
