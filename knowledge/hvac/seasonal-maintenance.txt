SEASONAL MAINTENANCE — WHAT A VISIT INCLUDES

WHAT A PRECISION TUNE-UP COVERS

A tune-up is a scheduled inspection and adjustment visit, not a repair visit. It
typically takes sixty to ninety minutes for a single residential system. The
technician performs the following:

Cooling tune-up (spring):
- Measure refrigerant pressures and compare against the manufacturer's charging
  chart for the current outdoor temperature.
- Clean the outdoor condenser coil and clear debris from around the unit.
- Inspect and clean the indoor evaporator coil where accessible.
- Flush and treat the condensate drain line, test the float safety switch.
- Measure the temperature difference across the indoor coil, which should
  generally fall between sixteen and twenty-two degrees Fahrenheit.
- Check capacitor microfarad values against rating and report any that have
  drifted more than ten percent, since a weak capacitor is the single most common
  cause of a mid-summer failure.
- Measure compressor and fan motor amp draw against nameplate.
- Tighten electrical connections and inspect contactor for pitting.
- Replace the standard filter and confirm airflow.
- Verify thermostat calibration and staging.

Heating tune-up (fall):
- Inspect the heat exchanger for cracks or corrosion. This is the most important
  safety check of the visit; a cracked heat exchanger can admit combustion gases
  into the airstream and the unit must be shut down until repaired or replaced.
- Test flame characteristics, ignition sequence, and flame sensor microamp signal.
- Measure gas pressure at the manifold and adjust to specification.
- Test all safety controls including the limit switch, rollout switch, and
  pressure switch.
- Inspect the flue and venting for blockage, corrosion, and proper draft.
- Measure temperature rise across the heat exchanger and compare to the nameplate
  range.
- Verify carbon monoxide levels in the supply air with a combustion analyzer.
- Clean the burners and blower assembly.
- Replace the standard filter and verify blower amp draw.
- Confirm thermostat operation in heating and, if present, correct heat pump
  changeover and auxiliary heat staging.

Commercial rooftop visit:
Adds belt tension and alignment, bearing lubrication where fittings exist,
economizer damper and actuator operation, outside-air setpoints, and a written
condition report per unit with photographs of any deficiency.

RECOMMENDED SCHEDULE

Cooling systems should be serviced in spring, before the first sustained stretch of
weather above eighty-five degrees. Heating systems should be serviced in fall,
before the first sustained stretch below fifty. Heat pumps, which run year round,
should be serviced twice a year regardless of plan. Commercial rooftop units
should be serviced at least twice a year and quarterly in dusty environments or
where filter loading is heavy.

Booking early in the season matters: the two weeks after the first heat wave and
the first hard freeze are consistently our busiest, and scheduled maintenance
slots fill first.

WHY MAINTENANCE MATTERS

The majority of no-heat and no-cool emergency calls we run are caused by
conditions a tune-up would have caught: a weak capacitor, a loaded filter, a
clogged condensate drain, a dirty flame sensor, a corroded contactor, or low
refrigerant from a slow leak. Manufacturers also require documented annual
maintenance to honor most parts warranties, so skipping it can void coverage on
equipment that is otherwise still under warranty.

WHAT HAPPENS IF WE FIND A PROBLEM

The technician reports the finding, quotes the repair, and lets the customer
decide. Nothing beyond the tune-up scope is performed without approval. The one
exception is a safety condition — a cracked heat exchanger, unsafe combustion
readings, or exposed live wiring — where the technician will red-tag and shut down
the equipment and explain why before leaving.
